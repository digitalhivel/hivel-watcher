# 93% of Developers Use AI. Why Is Productivity Up Only 10%?

More than nine in ten developers now use AI coding assistants. The average team member has used one in the last 30 days. A quarter of production code is AI-written.

And productivity — measured as actual engineering output that ships — is up about 10%.

That gap is the most important number in engineering right now. Not because it means AI does not work. Because it means most teams are measuring the wrong thing and cannot see why it is not working.

## The adoption-productivity gap is real, and your organization is probably in it

The latest research from Laura Tacho and the Clockwork team surveyed over 500 engineering organizations. Their finding: productivity gains from AI tools plateaued in Q2 2025 and have not moved since.

92.6% of developers use AI assistants monthly. 75% use them weekly. 26.9% of production code is now AI-authored. Time savings hover at about four hours per week per developer, unchanged for a year.

### What the latest data actually shows

Controlled research from METR — the organization that runs frontier AI safety evaluations — found a consistent pattern. Developers estimated they were 20% faster using AI tools. Controlled experiments measured something closer to 5-10%.

The perception gap matters. Teams are reporting high adoption to leadership and assuming it translates to impact. Often it does not.

### Why the 93% number is misleading

Adoption measures whether someone opened a tool. It does not measure whether using that tool shipped better software.

This sounds obvious until you see what most organizations actually track: acceptance rate (how often a developer clicked "accept"), active users per week, seat utilization. Activity metrics. They describe the tool. They say nothing about the outcome.

When 93% of developers use AI and productivity is still 10%, the problem is not adoption. Adoption is solved.

## Three reasons AI adoption does not convert to productivity

### You are measuring what is easy, not what matters

AI tool vendors surface acceptance rate by default. GitHub Copilot shows it. Amazon Q shows it. Most teams report it to their boards.

But a developer can accept 20 suggestions in a morning and commit none of them. Or accept a suggestion, immediately rewrite 80% of it, and ship the rewrite. The acceptance rate counter goes up either way.

Traditional metrics compound this. PR count, lines of code, commit frequency all inflate with AI assistance. More code gets written faster. But writing code was never the actual bottleneck for most engineering teams.

### Organizational context is the bottleneck, not the tool

The Clockwork research identified a sharp divide. Organizations with strong foundational engineering practices — fast CI, clear documentation, well-defined service boundaries — see AI as a force multiplier. 50% incident reductions. Real throughput gains.

Organizations without that foundation see the opposite. Twice as many customer-facing incidents. Output that inflates on dashboards but does not ship reliably.

The technology is identical. The outcomes are not. The difference is organizational readiness, not tool selection.

### The gap between accepted suggestions and shipped code

This is the metric almost no team tracks: what percentage of AI-authored code actually reaches production.

Teams we have seen at this stage believe they have strong AI adoption. The acceptance rates are 70-85%. When we trace that code from suggestion to production merge, the picture changes fast.

At one 500-person organization with $2 million in annual AI tooling investment, only 12% of AI-suggested code was production-merged six weeks in. The code was being accepted. It was not shipping.

Sudheer Matta, Hivel's CEO, has tracked this pattern across hundreds of teams: "85% of organizations think they have high AI adoption. The production-merge reality is closer to 18%. The gap between those two numbers is where the ROI disappears."

## What happens when you chase adoption instead of outcomes

### Metrics that look good on a dashboard but mean nothing

Here is what the board presentation looks like at most organizations right now: AI seat utilization up 40%, acceptance rate 78%, developers reporting four hours saved per week. The story looks good.

Here is what is happening underneath: deployment frequency unchanged, lead time unchanged, code review queue grown 60% because AI-generated PRs arrive faster than the team can review them.

At Linear, the engineering team saw this directly. Monthly PRs per author went from 40 to 59 after adopting agentic tools — a 48% increase. Code review became the new bottleneck. The team is now solving a governance problem, not a productivity problem.

### How chasing acceptance rate creates measurement debt

When you optimize for accepted suggestions, you build a measurement system that hides outcomes. Code that is accepted and rewritten counts as adoption. Code that is accepted and reverted counts as adoption. Code that ships a bug to production counts as adoption.

The metric gives you confidence you have not earned. Confidence without accuracy is more dangerous than no data at all.

## What actually predicts AI ROI in engineering

### The production-merge rate: the number nobody is tracking

Production-merge rate measures what percentage of AI-authored code reaches production. Not what gets accepted. Not what gets committed. What ships.

This is harder to track than acceptance rate. It requires attributing code to its origin, following it through the full review cycle, and measuring how much reaches the main branch without being significantly rewritten.

Teams that have done this work report a consistent finding: production-merge rate predicts actual productivity improvement far better than any usage metric.

### Why team structure matters more than tool selection

Uber spent two years trying to measure AI's impact. Their initial metric — "developer years saved" — seemed compelling and created anxiety about job replacement without answering the business question.

They landed on feature velocity: did valuable capabilities actually ship? Not was code written. Not were agents busy. Did the product improve?

That shift required organizational clarity about what "done" actually meant. A question most teams have not answered precisely enough for AI measurement to work.

### The three organizational conditions that actually work

The Clockwork research identified three prerequisites for AI to translate into real gains.

Clear goals and measurement systems. Teams that define what "improved" looks like before deploying AI tools see dramatically better outcomes than those who deploy first and measure later.

Strong developer experience infrastructure. Fast CI, clear documentation, well-defined services. Without these, AI amplifies friction rather than reducing it. Code gets written faster. The downstream bottlenecks get worse faster.

Active change management. 38% of AI implementation difficulty is user proficiency — the learning curve and prompt engineering gaps — versus 16% for technical issues. The people challenge is real and most organizations are not addressing it directly.

## What elite teams are doing differently

### Moving from activity metrics to outcome metrics

AvidXChange saw PR cycle time drop 56% in six months. That improvement did not come from accepting more suggestions. It came from identifying where AI-generated code was getting stuck in review and fixing those specific stages.

They measured backward from outcomes. Most organizations measure forward from activity.

### How Uber, Airbnb, and Linear changed their measurement approach

Linear built measurement alongside the agentic tooling. When PR counts inflated, they asked which PRs were immediately mergeable versus which needed multiple review cycles. Over 50% of agent-generated PRs were immediately mergeable — a data point that actually maps to quality, not just volume.

Airbnb reached 97% weekly AI usage without mandates. They got there by investing in the developer experience of the AI tools themselves: onboarding, community-driven learning, making agentic workflows native rather than bolted on. Usage followed naturally. Then they measured a 65% PR throughput increase — and immediately started asking whether that throughput was translating to shipped features.

The pattern is consistent. Elite teams measure one layer deeper than their peers.

## Should you change how you measure AI? The decision framework

If your AI metrics are adoption-only, you are measuring the wrong layer. The acceptance rate does not matter.

The question to ask: what percentage of AI-authored code in your codebase reached production without significant rewriting? If you do not know that number, you are flying blind on an investment that almost certainly exceeds $1M annually at any engineering team of scale.

The measurement system matters as much as the tool. Teams that build outcome measurement alongside AI deployment see sustained productivity gains. Teams that measure adoption and call it done plateau at 10% and stay there.

## FAQ

**Why do developer surveys show 20% productivity gains but controlled experiments show 10%?**

Developers estimate improvement based on how specific tasks feel. Controlled experiments measure end-to-end throughput including review, testing, and deployment. The gap between how fast code gets written and how fast software ships is larger than most developers account for in self-reporting.

**If 93% of developers use AI, why is only 10% productivity gain visible?**

Adoption reduces friction on one task: writing code. It leaves intact all the downstream bottlenecks — code review, testing, deployment, debugging production incidents. Productivity at the team level requires addressing the whole system, not one step.

**What is a good production-merge rate for AI-authored code?**

Teams performing well typically see 30-50% of AI-authored code reaching production in recognizable form. Below 20% indicates significant friction in review or quality issues requiring heavy rewriting. Above 60% may indicate insufficient review, which tends to show up as incident increases downstream.

**Should we stop using AI adoption as a metric?**

No. Adoption is a leading indicator — it tells you whether the capability is in use at all. The problem is using it as a lagging indicator of success. Track adoption to confirm the tools are being used. Track production-merge rate, deployment frequency changes, and incident rate to know whether the tools are working.

**How long before real productivity gains show up from AI coding tools?**

Organizations with strong foundations — fast CI, clear docs, good service design — typically see measurable throughput improvement within 8-12 weeks. Organizations working through foundational issues first often need 6+ months before AI contributes net-positive productivity. The bottleneck is almost never the tool itself.
