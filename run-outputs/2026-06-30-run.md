## The numbers look good. The code might not be.

Your team is shipping faster than ever. PR volume is up. Lead time is down. The board wants to know how you did it.

Here is what the board is not seeing.

Teams using AI agents are shipping five times more pull requests than they were two years ago, according to data from Linear. Developers produce 2.5 times more code than they did 18 months ago (Cursor metrics). Pull request sizes have tripled in the same window.

That is the speed side. The quality side is harder to find in your dashboards.

What does a 3x increase in PR size mean for review quality? Senior engineers are looking at more code per review than ever, while the organizations running the heaviest AI workflows have simultaneously reduced human review requirements. The result is not faster, better code. It is faster code with fewer eyes on it.

This is not a future risk. It is happening now.

### What 5x PR volume actually means for your team

Volume is not the problem. Unreviewed volume is the problem.

A team that ships 5x more PRs with 5x more human review hours has a reasonable chance of catching problems. A team that ships 5x more PRs while keeping review time flat has a review debt problem that will surface in production.

Here is the pattern teams we have worked with recognize: deployment frequency goes up, bugs go up six weeks later. The gap between the two metrics is the time it takes for AI-generated code to reach production, get adopted, and start surfacing edge cases that no one in the review cycle caught.

Deployment frequency up 50%, bugs up 80%. That is not faster. That is breaking things faster.

The Klenty team saw the opposite. When they implemented systematic code review with AI-assisted first-pass coverage, cycle time came down 25%, review time dropped 22%, and feature output increased 49%. The difference was not removing review. It was making review intelligent enough to scale with velocity.

### Why PR size tripling is a review quality signal

A pull request that is three times larger is not three times more reviewable. Human cognitive capacity for code review does not scale linearly with PR size. At a certain point, reviewers shift from detailed review to pattern matching — trusting that if the code looks structurally correct it probably works.

AI-generated code is very good at looking structurally correct.

The problem is that it often lacks the implicit context that human-written code carries. A human engineer who has been in the codebase for 18 months knows that the authentication middleware has a specific assumption baked in from a 2024 incident. They write around it without thinking. An AI agent does not know what it does not know.

When PR size triples and human review time stays flat, that contextual knowledge is the first thing that stops getting checked.

## What happens when AI reviews AI

The most direct case study for this risk appeared in June 2026 at Meta.

Gergely Orosz at The Pragmatic Engineer documented a significant breach in Meta's Instagram platform. AI-generated code, reviewed primarily by AI, implemented a "zero auth" policy that allowed users to change any account's email address without verification. The vulnerability was exploited at scale.

The root causes were not mysterious:
- AI-generated code without adequate human oversight
- Security and integrity teams cut by 50% through layoffs and reassignments
- Review processes that relied on AI to catch what AI had written

This is not an argument against AI. Meta's engineering organization is among the most sophisticated in the world. This is an argument about what happens when speed optimization and staffing decisions outpace the review infrastructure needed to catch the errors that speed creates.

### The silent pattern: no outage, but mounting technical debt

The Meta breach was visible. Most quality degradation under AI velocity is not.

What you are more likely to see is a 20% increase in bug tickets three months after deploying AI coding agents. A slower-than-expected velocity in Q3 when your engineers are spending 40% of their time refactoring AI-generated code that passed initial review but does not hold up in production load testing. A change failure rate that creeps up slowly enough that no single week looks alarming.

Teams we have analyzed show this pattern consistently. The AI ROI story looks strong in month one and month two. By month four, the rework costs start showing up, and nobody has a clean way to attribute them back to the original AI output.

That is a measurement problem. You cannot fix what you cannot see.

## The measurement gap engineering leaders are walking into

DORA metrics will not show you this.

Deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to recovery measure what your delivery system does. They do not tell you what percentage of your delivery is AI-generated, or whether AI-generated code has a different failure rate than human-written code.

That is the data most teams do not have.

Oobeya published research in March 2026 showing that elite engineering teams now operate on a four-layer measurement stack: the traditional DORA baseline, developer experience metrics, AI attribution data, and business alignment metrics. The AI attribution layer is the new requirement. Most organizations do not have it.

### Five signals your code quality is degrading under AI speed

If you do not have AI attribution data yet, these are the proxy signals to watch:

**Change failure rate climbing slowly, not in spikes.** A sudden spike suggests a deployment error. A slow climb over 8-12 weeks suggests a systemic quality issue. AI-generated code that lacks contextual awareness tends to produce the slow climb.

**Senior engineer review time increasing.** If your experienced engineers are spending more time in review than they were six months ago despite the team shipping more overall, something is wrong with the code quality arriving for their attention.

**Bug tickets classified as "unexpected behavior."** Bugs that are clearly wrong get caught in review. Bugs that are subtly wrong because they lack contextual awareness tend to manifest as unexpected behavior in edge cases. Watch the proportion of your bug tracker that falls into this category.

**Rework rate increasing quarter-over-quarter.** The 2025 DORA report added rework rate as a fifth metric for exactly this reason. Rework tracks how much engineering time is reactive rather than planned. A rising rework rate following high AI adoption is the signal most teams miss until it is expensive.

**PR review time per PR decreasing while PR size increases.** If your team is doing bigger PRs faster, they are doing less rigorous review. That will show up in production eventually. It always does.

## Building the right review architecture for AI-generated code

The answer is not removing AI from your development workflow. The answer is building the review architecture that makes AI velocity sustainable.

### Human review touchpoints that do not become bottlenecks

The reason organizations reduce human review is that it becomes a bottleneck. Five times the PR volume with the same review team creates a queue problem. Most teams reach for the wrong solution: reducing review requirements.

The right solution is different.

An AI-assisted first-pass review that catches 60-70% of issues before a human sees the PR does not remove the human review requirement. It transforms it. Instead of reading every line, senior engineers read diffs that already have a quality baseline applied. Review time per PR goes down without review depth going down.

Hivel's AI Code Review Agent applies first-pass coverage across every PR, filtering out the routine issues so senior engineers spend their time on the contextual judgment calls that AI cannot yet make. At Klenty, that meant review time dropped 22% while feature output went up 49%. At Shiprocket, a unicorn with significant engineering complexity, bugs in production came down 22%. The review step did not disappear. It became useful again instead of a queue.

### What production-level AI attribution tells you that PR metrics cannot

Accepted suggestions is not shipped code. This is the metric gap that creates the most confusion.

Your AI coding tool reports an acceptance rate. It looks like a productivity metric. It is not. It tells you what your developers clicked "accept" on. It does not tell you what made it through review, what was merged to main, what deployed to production, or what is holding up three months later.

Production-level AI attribution tracks what percentage of code in production today started as AI-generated output. Not what was accepted. Not what was in a branch. What is actually running.

Teams with this data consistently find the same thing: production-merged AI code share is significantly lower than their acceptance rate suggests. The gap is code that was accepted, then edited, then partially reverted, then replaced in a follow-up PR. That gap is where the rework cost lives.

AvidXChange used Hivel data to identify where in their development pipeline cycle time was actually being lost. PR cycle time came down 56% in six months. Not by shipping faster. By seeing clearly where the work was stacking up and fixing the actual bottleneck.

## FAQ

**Does implementing AI code review slow down development?**

Done correctly, no. AI-assisted first-pass review filters the issues that create the most review friction, reducing back-and-forth between author and reviewer. Teams that implement it well see review time decrease while review coverage increases. The slowdown happens when review is treated as a bottleneck to eliminate rather than a quality control to make intelligent.

**What is AI code churn rate and why does it matter?**

AI code churn rate measures what percentage of AI-generated code is modified or deleted within a short window after being merged. High churn indicates that AI output is not landing cleanly — it requires immediate follow-up to correct, extend, or clean up. Teams with high AI acceptance rates but also high AI code churn are generating AI output at volume without much of it sticking. That gap is the rework cost that does not show up in your velocity metrics.

**How many engineering teams are tracking AI attribution today?**

Very few. The Oobeya research in March 2026 identified AI attribution as a required fourth layer for elite engineering teams, but most organizations are still running on DORA alone. The 2025 DORA report added rework rate as a fifth metric, which is a partial proxy, but dedicated AI attribution (code share, churn rate, acceptance-to-production gap) is not yet standard practice.

**Is this primarily a security concern, or does quality degradation affect other areas?**

Security is the most visible failure mode because breaches have external consequences. But quality degradation under AI velocity affects reliability (change failure rate), developer experience (rework cycles, mounting technical debt), and business outcomes (slower feature delivery in quarters following high AI adoption, as rework crowds out new work). The Meta breach is the example that made the news. Most organizations are experiencing the same dynamic at lower severity across all three dimensions simultaneously.

**What is the right ratio of AI-generated to human-written code?**

There is no universal ratio. The right question is not what percentage is AI-generated but whether your review and measurement infrastructure scales with whatever percentage you have chosen. A team shipping 80% AI-generated code with robust first-pass review, production attribution tracking, and senior engineer review capacity for the remaining judgment calls can manage that ratio well. The same team shipping 40% AI-generated code without any of that infrastructure is carrying more risk than it looks.
